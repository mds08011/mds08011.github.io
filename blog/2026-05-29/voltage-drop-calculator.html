Circular-Mil Voltage Drop for Temporary Power Runs

May 2026

Overview

Long temporary electrical runs, such as feeders from a generator or distribution panel to distant equipment, lose voltage along the cable due to conductor resistance. Reduced voltage at a motor causes it to draw higher current to deliver the same mechanical output, increasing heat in the motor windings. Excessive voltage drop can also cause variable frequency drives (VFDs, electronic motor speed controllers) or magnetic contactor coils (motor starter switches) to trip. This calculator estimates voltage drop, percent drop, load-end voltage, and maximum one-way cable length at a 3% drop limit for single-phase and three-phase circuits.

Formulas

The tool uses the standard circular-mil voltage-drop formula:

Three-phase: V_d = √3 × K × I × L / CM
Single-phase: V_d = 2 × K × I × L / CM

Here V_d is voltage drop in volts (V), K is conductor resistivity in ohm-circular-mils per foot (Ω·cmil/ft, taken as 12.9 for copper and 21.2 for aluminum at operating temperature), I is load current in amperes (A), L is one-way circuit length in feet (ft), and CM is the conductor cross-sectional area in circular mils (cmil). One circular mil is the area of a circle 0.001 inch in diameter. Selecting a wire size in American Wire Gauge (AWG, where smaller gauge numbers mean thicker wire) or kcmil (thousands of circular mils) fills in the corresponding area from National Electrical Code (NEC) Chapter 9 Table 8 [1]. When parallel conductors per phase are entered, the tool multiplies CM by the number of parallel sets.

The calculator compares percent voltage drop against the NEC Articles 210 and 215 informational notes, which recommend limiting voltage drop to 3% for a single branch circuit or feeder and 5% total combined drop from source to load for reasonable efficiency of operation [1]. These percentages are non-mandatory recommendations rather than code requirements. For quick lookup of motor loads, the tool includes a reference table of NEC Table 430.250 full-load amperes (FLA, rated running current) for 460 V three-phase motors by horsepower (HP), such as 34 A for 25 HP and 124 A for 100 HP, since NEC conductor sizing uses table FLA values rather than motor nameplate current [1].

Assumptions and limits
  • The circular-mil formula accounts only for conductor resistance and neglects inductive reactance (opposition to alternating current from magnetic fields around the conductors). This approximation is most accurate at high power factor (the ratio of real power to apparent power) and on smaller to mid-sized conductors. Larger cables and lower-power-factor motor loads can have higher actual voltage drop.
  • Across-the-line motor starting draws roughly six times FLA briefly, so a cable run with acceptable running voltage drop can still experience a much larger temporary drop during motor startup.
  • This calculator estimates voltage drop only. Conductor ampacity (safe continuous current capacity), temperature or bundling derating, overcurrent protection (breakers or fuses), grounding, and any permanent electrical installation require a licensed electrician or electrical engineer of record.

The calculator runs offline in a single HTML file using plain JavaScript with no external libraries, and saves your inputs in your browser's local storage (localStorage).
