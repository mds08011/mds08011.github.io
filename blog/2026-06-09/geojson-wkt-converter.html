Converting and Inspecting GeoJSON, WKT, and CSV Geometry

June 2026

This utility converts geographic coordinates among GeoJSON (a JSON-based format defined by RFC 7946 [1]), WKT (Well-Known Text, a plain-text geometry format from the Open Geospatial Consortium Simple Features specification [2]), and CSV (comma-separated values, with one coordinate pair per line). It also reports the vertex count (number of corner points), bounding box (West, South, East, and North coordinate limits), and approximate spherical length and area. All parsing and math run locally in the browser without uploading coordinate data to a remote server.

The tool supports six standard 2D geometry types: Point, MultiPoint, LineString, MultiLineString, Polygon, and MultiPolygon (it does not support GeometryCollection). For GeoJSON input, it accepts a bare geometry object, a Feature, or a FeatureCollection (reading the first feature's geometry and noting when additional features were skipped), and warns if a deprecated crs coordinate-system member is present because RFC 7946 assumes WGS84 (World Geodetic System 1984) longitude and latitude in decimal degrees [1]. For WKT input, it parses coordinate lists case-insensitively and ignores extra numeric values such as elevation (Z) or measure (M) coordinates [2]. For CSV input, it skips a non-numeric header row, lets you choose lon,lat or lat,lon column order, and interprets rows as a line, an auto-closed polygon, or loose points.

GeoJSON and WKT both specify coordinates in longitude-first order (lon, lat) [1][2], whereas many spreadsheets and GPS exports list latitude first (lat, lon). Valid latitude stays within ±90° while longitude ranges up to ±180°, so reading latitude-first data as longitude-first often places a longitude magnitude over 90 into the latitude position (for example, swapping a California coordinate pair near 34°, -118° produces an invalid latitude of -118°). The tool checks whether any latitude exceeds ±90° while all longitudes remain within ±90° and prints a warning when coordinates appear swapped.

Assumptions and limits

Line lengths and polygon perimeters are summed across consecutive vertices using the haversine great-circle distance formula (the shortest path on a sphere): d = 2·R·atan2(√a, √(1 − a)), where a = sin²((φ₂ − φ₁) / 2) + cos(φ₁)·cos(φ₂)·sin²((λ₂ − λ₁) / 2). Here φ₁, φ₂ are latitudes in radians, λ₁, λ₂ are longitudes in radians, and R = 6,371,008.8 m is the mean Earth radius from the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics (IUGG) [4].

Polygon area uses the Chamberlain and Duquette spherical ring formula [3]: A = (R² / 2) · |Σ (λ₂ − λ₁) · (2 + sin φ₁ + sin φ₂)| in square meters, summed over consecutive ring edges with interior hole rings subtracted from the exterior ring. Spherical calculations typically stay within about 0.3% of ellipsoidal values at mid-latitudes, which is fine for quick inspection and quality-assurance checks, while survey or legal record boundaries require ellipsoidal calculations in Geographic Information System (GIS) or survey software.

The page stores your last input in browser storage (localStorage) and provides copy buttons for all three output formats. It can also be used alongside the bearing-distance traverse calculator and the coordinate converter.
