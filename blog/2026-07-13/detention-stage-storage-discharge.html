Detention Basin Stage-Storage-Discharge Calculator

July 2026

A detention basin holds stormwater runoff temporarily and releases it at a controlled rate. Before routing a design storm through a basin, you need a stage-storage-discharge table that lists the stored volume and total outlet flow at each water-surface elevation (stage). This calculator builds that table from basin geometry and up to two outlets, plots both curves against elevation, and copies the result as CSV (comma-separated values) text for spreadsheets or hydrology software.

Storage is entered either as a stage-area survey (water-surface area at each elevation) or as a rectangular prism with sloped sides. Between two adjacent stages with surface areas A1 and A2 (ft²) separated by an elevation step Δh (ft), the tool computes incremental volume by the average-end-area method: ΔV = ((A1 + A2) / 2) · Δh, where ΔV is in cubic feet (ft³). Volumes are summed from the lowest elevation upward and reported in ft³ and acre-feet (1 ac-ft = 43,560 ft³) [1].

Each outlet has an invert or crest elevation (the lowest elevation where water starts leaving) and contributes flow only when the water surface is above it. For a circular orifice (a submerged opening), free-discharge flow is Q = Cd · A · √(2 · g · h), where Q is discharge in cubic feet per second (cfs), Cd is the dimensionless discharge coefficient (default 0.60), A is the opening area (ft², from diameter in inches), g is gravitational acceleration (32.174 ft/s²), and h is head from the water surface to the orifice centerline (ft) [3]. For a rectangular weir (a flat-crested barrier water spills over), flow is Q = Cw · L · h^1.5, where Cw is the weir coefficient (default 3.33 in US customary units), L is crest length (ft), and h is water depth above the crest (ft) [3]. A 90-degree V-notch weir option uses Q = 2.49 · h^2.48 with h in ft and Q in cfs [3].

In Figure 1, only the low orifice flows between elevations 100 ft and 104 ft, where discharge increases with h^0.5. Above elevation 104 ft, the overflow weir begins discharging at h^1.5, creating a visible slope change in the discharge curve. This table is an input to reservoir routing, not routing itself. Routing an inflow hydrograph (runoff flow rate over time) requires a separate method such as storage-indication (modified Puls) routing, which steps through time using 2S/Δt + Q, where S is storage (ft³), Δt is the time step (s), and Q is outflow (cfs) [1], [2].

Assumptions and limits
  • Average-end-area vs. conic method: The average-end-area formula ((A1 + A2) / 2) · Δh slightly overestimates volume for a sloped basin compared to the conic (frustum) formula ((A1 + A2 + √(A1 · A2)) / 3) · Δh. Accuracy also depends directly on the surveyed stage-area elevations.
  • Free discharge only: The outlet equations assume water discharges freely to the atmosphere. If downstream tailwater (water level beyond the outlet) rises above a weir crest or orifice, submerged weir or submerged orifice flow reduces capacity below the values shown here [3].
  • Debris and local standards: Outlet clogging is not modeled. Check the governing local drainage manual for required discharge coefficients, minimum orifice diameters, trash racks (debris screens), and freeboard.
  • Related tools: Use the detention basin estimator for a preliminary volume estimate and the weir and orifice flow calculator for checking a single outlet structure.
  • Local storage: Inputs are saved in your browser's localStorage (stored locally on your device) and can be cleared with the reset button.
