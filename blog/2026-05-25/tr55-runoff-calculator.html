Estimating Storm Runoff with the TR-55 Curve Number Method

May 2026

Overview

This calculator estimates storm runoff depth and total runoff volume for a small watershed using the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) curve number method from Technical Release 55 (TR-55) [1]. The method combines land cover and soil drainage properties into a single dimensionless curve number (CN, typically 30 to 98) and converts a 24-hour design storm depth (total rainfall for a selected return period, such as from NOAA Atlas 14 [3]) into direct runoff.

Formulas

The TR-55 runoff equations are [1][2]:

Q = (P − Ia)² / (P − Ia + S) for P > Ia (and Q = 0 when P ≤ Ia)
S = 1000 / CN − 10 and Ia = 0.2 × S

Here Q is runoff depth in inches, P is 24-hour rainfall depth in inches, S is potential maximum retention (the maximum water depth the soil and cover can absorb after runoff begins) in inches, and Ia is initial abstraction (rainfall lost to surface wetting, depressions, and early infiltration before runoff starts) in inches. To build a composite curve number, the tool provides editable TR-55 Table 2-2 presets across twelve land-use categories and hydrologic soil groups (HSG) A through D, where group A is well-drained sandy soil and group D is poorly drained clay soil obtained from the NRCS Web Soil Survey [1][4]. It converts runoff depth across the total acreage into acre-feet, cubic feet, and gallons.

Area-weighting curve numbers across mixed surfaces has an important limitation noted in TR-55 [1]. When a site mixes low-CN pervious surfaces (areas water can soak into, such as grass or woods) with impervious surfaces (hard surfaces such as roofs or pavement), blending them into a single composite CN can understate runoff, especially for small storms or when impervious areas drain directly to pipes. When impervious areas are unconnected (draining across pervious ground first), TR-55 Figures 2-3 and 2-4 provide composite CN adjustments [1]. For better accuracy on mixed sites, runoff can be computed separately for each cover type and the resulting volumes summed. A site with connected impervious area can still produce runoff even when a composite CN yields P ≤ Ia and zero computed runoff.

Assumptions and limits
  • Table 2-2 curve numbers assume average antecedent runoff condition (ARC II, meaning average soil moisture before the storm). Conversions for dry (ARC I) and wet (ARC III) conditions are documented in NRCS National Engineering Handbook Part 630 [2]. Check the local drainage manual for required conditions and local CN tables.
  • Because the curve number equation is empirical, very small computed runoff depths are unreliable. The calculator displays a warning when Q is below 0.05 inches.
  • This tool computes runoff depth and volume only. Estimating peak discharge rate requires time of concentration (Tc, the travel time from the farthest point in the watershed to the outlet), the regional 24-hour rainfall distribution type, and the TR-55 graphical or tabular peak-discharge method (or a hydrologic model) [1].

The calculator runs offline in a single HTML file using plain JavaScript with no external libraries, and saves your inputs in your browser's local storage (localStorage).
