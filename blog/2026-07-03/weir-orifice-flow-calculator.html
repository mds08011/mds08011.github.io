Temporary Flow Measurement with Weirs and Orifices

July 2026

A sharp-crested weir (a thin vertical plate with a notch cut into the top edge) or a submerged orifice (a submerged opening of known area) estimates open-channel flow from a water-level measurement when no permanent flow meter is present. This calculator converts measured head (water height above the weir crest or pressure-level difference across an orifice) into flow rate in cubic feet per second (cfs), gallons per minute (GPM), and million gallons per day (MGD).

The calculator uses standard US customary weir and orifice discharge equations from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) Water Measurement Manual [1] (thin-plate weir methods are also standardized in ISO 1438 [2]):

90° V-notch (USBR):        Q = 2.49 · h^2.48
V-notch, other angles:     Q = 2.5 · tan(θ/2) · h^2.5
Rectangular, contracted:   Q = 3.33 · (L − 0.2h) · h^1.5   (Francis)
Rectangular, suppressed:   Q = 3.33 · L · h^1.5
Cipolletti:                Q = 3.367 · L · h^1.5
Submerged orifice:         Q = C_d · A · √(2gΔh)

Here Q is flow rate in cfs, h is water head measured upstream above the weir crest or V-notch vertex in feet (entered in inches in the tool and converted internally), θ is the V-notch angle in degrees (90°, 60°, or 45° presets), L is horizontal crest length in feet, C_d is the dimensionless orifice discharge coefficient (default 0.61 for a sharp-edged opening), A is net open orifice area in square feet (entered in square inches in the tool), Δh is differential head across the submerged orifice in feet (upstream water elevation minus downstream water elevation, entered in inches), and g = 32.174 ft/s² is gravitational acceleration.

In a contracted rectangular weir, the notch is narrower than the channel, so water narrows (contracts) at both ends and the Francis term (L − 0.2h) accounts for those end contractions [1]. A suppressed rectangular weir spans the full channel width to eliminate side contractions, while a Cipolletti weir uses trapezoidal 1 horizontal to 4 vertical side slopes whose extra area compensates for end contractions [1]. V-notch weirs give better sensitivity at low or variable flows. Rectangular and Cipolletti weirs suit higher flows where a V-notch would require excessive head. A submerged orifice is used when there is not enough drop for water to fall freely over a weir.

Assumptions and limits
  • Head measurement and range: Measure head h upstream of the weir (at least 4h back from the crest) rather than at the crest where surface drawdown occurs. Most sharp-crested weir equations are rated for roughly 0.2 to 2.0 ft of head. Below about 0.2 ft, surface tension causes the falling sheet of water (the nappe) to cling to the plate, and the tool displays a warning [1][2].
  • Free (aerated) nappe: Air must circulate underneath the falling sheet of water. A clinging nappe causes the equation to overestimate flow, so full-width suppressed rectangular weirs require side air vents [1].
  • Approach and downstream conditions: The equations assume a calm upstream weir pool (low approach velocity), free discharge with no downstream submergence (downstream water level remains below the crest), and a crest height at least 2h above the upstream channel bottom [1].
  • Crest geometry: A sharp-crested weir requires a thin, square upstream edge (such as ¼-in. plate beveled downstream). A thick crest such as 2× lumber behaves as a broad-crested weir with a different discharge coefficient.
  • Accuracy: Properly installed temporary weirs are typically accurate to around ±5% and are suited for short-term verification rather than billing or permit reporting, which require a calibrated flume or permanent meter [1].

The calculator runs in the browser and saves inputs in browser local storage (localStorage, a built-in browser store on the current device) until cleared.
