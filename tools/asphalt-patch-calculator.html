Hot mix tonnage for trench restoration and patches: base and surface courses, T-patch cutback per the encroachment permit, waste factor, and truckloads. Runs entirely offline; inputs persist in this browser via localStorage.

Use limits: Tonnage assumes compacted, in-place density on plan dimensions. Ragged saw cuts, over-milling, subgrade undulation, and hand work around castings all eat mix beyond neat-line — that's what the waste factor is for, and on small patches it's a big percentage. Mix type, lift thicknesses, tack, and the cutback dimension come from the agency's encroachment permit / standard details, which govern.

How the Numbers Are Derived

The math

tons = area (sf) × depth (in) / 12 × density (pcf) / 2000

A convenient cross-check: at 145 pcf, one ton covers about 16.5 sf per inch of compacted depth — or ~110 lb per square yard per inch (the "110 rule" estimators use).

For the T-patch, the base course runs at trench width and the surface course at trench width plus the cutback on each side. The pay area shown is the surface (widest) footprint in SF and SY.

Field notes
  • Small patches have brutal waste percentages — a 10-ton minimum load against a 6-ton patch is 40% "waste" no matter how careful the crew is. Batch nearby patches into one paving day.
  • HMA loses workability with temperature; a long haul or a slow crew on a cold day means more raked-off, unusable mix. That's waste factor too.
  • Confirm whether the permit measures the patch by surface area (SY) or by tonnage tickets — keep the delivery tickets either way.
  • Milling the cutback instead of a second saw cut usually gives a better joint and a cleaner tack surface — if the permit allows it.
