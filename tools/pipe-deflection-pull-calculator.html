How the Numbers Are Derived

Deflection, radius, and offset

Each straight pipe is a chord of the curve, kinked at the joint by the deflection angle θ. A chord of length L on a circle of radius R spans L = 2R · sin(θ/2), so the minimum radius from an allowable θ is R = L / (2 sin(θ/2)), and the deflection needed for a target radius is θ = 2 · asin(L / 2R). The lateral offset each pipe gains over the last is L · sin(θ). To turn a total angle, the joints needed are total / θ (round up), and the pipe length is joints × L.

Field notes
  • Deflect at the joint by pulling the spigot to one side of the bell — not by bowing the pipe barrel, which over-stresses it and isn't what the allowable covers.
  • Allowables shrink as pipe gets bigger and as the joint gets more restrained; a 5° push-on 8-inch is generous, a restrained 24-inch is a degree or two. Always pull the manufacturer's table for the exact joint and class.
  • On a pressure line, deflection and pull-out eat into the joint's safety margin against separation — thrust restraint still has to be designed for the bends and the deflected run.
  • Vertical curves (over a crossing, following a grade) use the same math; just remember gravity-sewer slope tolerances are tight, so a deflected sewer still has to hold its grade.
  • Gaining a little length by pulling joints is legitimate for hitting a fitting or a structure, but spread it — one joint pulled to its limit is a leak risk; a fraction of the pull on several is not.
